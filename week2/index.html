01 · NETWORK FOUNDATIONS

Networks connect actors through relationships

Nodes

Also called actors, agents, or vertices. Nodes may be individuals, teams, organizations, content, locations, or events.

  • Attributes describe nodes: age, role, organization, activity.
  • Size, color, and shape can encode attributes in a graph.

Edges

Also called ties, connections, or links. Edges describe a relationship between two nodes.

  • Arrows encode direction.
  • Thickness or darkness can encode strength.
Analytical choice

The same setting can produce different networks. On X, nodes might be users and edges follows, mentions, or retweets. Your definition changes the research question.

02 · NETWORK TYPES

Direction and weight create four basic network types

UNDIRECTED · UNWEIGHTED

Friendship

A tie either exists or does not, with no origin or destination.

UNDIRECTED · WEIGHTED

Co-authorship

A shared paper connects two authors; weight can count papers.

DIRECTED · UNWEIGHTED

Following

A follows B does not require B to follow A.

DIRECTED · WEIGHTED

Communication

Messages have a sender, receiver, and frequency.

04 · NETWORK-LEVEL METRICS

Size defines the boundary; density describes connectedness

N

Network size

The number of nodes included within the network boundary.

D

Density

The proportion of all possible ties that are actually present.

Undirected: D = 2L / N(N−1)

Try the density calculator

For an undirected network, enter the number of nodes and observed links.

D = 0.60
Interpret carefully

Networks of different sizes have different numbers of possible ties. Density allows a relative comparison, but boundaries and network type must be comparable.

05 · PATHS AND DISTANCE

Indirect connections reveal reachability

Path

A sequence that does not revisit the same node.

Walk

Any sequence; nodes may be revisited.

Distance

Edges in the shortest path between two nodes.

Diameter

Maximum distance in a connected network.

Connected graph

07 · DEGREE CENTRALITY

Who has the most direct connections?

Degree centrality is the number of neighbors directly connected to a node. In a directed network:

  • Indegree: incoming ties; often popularity or prestige.
  • Outdegree: outgoing ties; often activity or outreach.

08 · CLOSENESS CENTRALITY

Who can reach everyone quickly?

A node has high closeness when its total distance to all other nodes is small.

  • Useful when speed of access or diffusion matters.
  • More diffusion steps may create delay or distortion.
CC(i) = 1 / Σ d(i,j)
Node ASum of distance = 5Closeness = .200
Node BSum of distance = 8Closeness = .125

A is closer because it has the smaller total distance.

09 · STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGE

Gatekeeping and prestige are not the same

BETWEENNESS

Control over flows

Counts how often a node falls on shortest paths. A broker may filter, delay, or reshape information.

EIGENVECTOR

Important neighbors

A node is important when it connects to important nodes. Connections are weighted by neighbors’ centrality.

10 · PAGERANK

Endorsement circulates through a directed network

1

Initialize

Give each of n nodes an initial score of 1/n.

2

Distribute

Divide each score across outgoing links.

3

Update

Sum all shares received by each node.

4

Repeat

Important nodes make stronger endorsements.

Why add random jumps?

Without them, PageRank may become trapped in a small set of nodes with no path back. A damping process redistributes some score.

THINK & DISCUSS

Is high centrality always good?

Consider visibility, access, overload, surveillance, responsibility, and control over flows.
